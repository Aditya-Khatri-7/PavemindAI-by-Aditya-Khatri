PavemindAI

An AI-powered system that detects potholes from road footage, grades how bad they are, and shows the results on a live dashboard with maps and analytics.

Monocular vision pipeline for automated pavement distress detection, metric severity estimation, and municipal repair prioritisation — built end-to-end as a deployable full-stack system.

01

The Pitch

Say this first Abstract framing

We built PavemindAI — a system that watches dashcam footage of a road and automatically finds every pothole in it. For each one it works out how serious it is, marks it on the video, and pushes everything to a web dashboard where you can see the totals, a health score for that road, and a map of where the problems are.

Then open the dashboard and run a live upload →

Road maintenance is reactive because surveying is manual and unquantified. Existing pothole detectors stop at a bounding box — they tell you where, never how bad, and a box in pixels is not actionable for a works department.

PavemindAI closes that gap. It couples a fine-tuned YOLOv12 detector with a monocular metric-estimation layer that recovers real-world pothole dimensions in centimetres from a single uncalibrated dashcam, then folds size, depth proxy, shape, confidence and temporal persistence into a weighted severity score — which aggregates into a 0–100 Road Health Index that a municipality can actually rank roads by.

Contribution = the layer above detection, not the detector

The Severity Engine

High level

Detecting a pothole is only half the problem — the system also has to judge how serious it is. The severity engine looks at several signals together (how large the pothole is in the real world, its shape, how confident the model is, and how consistently it appears across frames) and combines them into a single score, which becomes the Minor / Moderate / Severe label.

Those labels then roll up into a Road Health Score out of 100 for the whole scan, so an entire stretch of road can be compared against another at a glance.

04

Severity Engine — Full Internals

Core contribution #1

Multi-factor weighted scoring

Severity is not thresholded on any single quantity. Five normalised factors, each in [0,1], are linearly combined:

S = 0.45·fsize + 0.20·fdepth + 0.15·fconf + 0.10·faspect + 0.10·fpersist
f_size — piecewise-linear ramp on estimated physical width (cm)
f_depth — same ramp on estimated physical height, used as a depth proxy
f_conf — raw YOLO confidence
f_aspect — 1.0 inside the ideal w/h band [1.0, 3.0], decaying outside
f_persist — min(frames_seen, 15) / 15 via ByteTrack ID history
ScoreClassMeaning
S ≥ 0.70SEVEREImmediate repair priority
0.45 ≤ S < 0.70MODERATEScheduled maintenance
S < 0.45MINORMonitor only
💡
Defence for the weights: physical size dominates at 0.45 because it's the only factor that maps to real-world repair cost. Persistence is low at 0.10 deliberately — it must nudge, never decide, or a slow-moving vehicle would inflate every score.

Monocular metric estimation — the actual novelty

Recovering real-world size from one camera with no stereo pair and no depth sensor. Uses a pinhole model plus a flat-ground assumption, treating the bounding box's bottom edge as the road contact point.

d = H / tan( θ + (y₂ − c_y)/f )
d — ground distance to pothole (m)
H = 1.3 m — camera height above road
θ = 18° — camera downward tilt from horizon
f = 800 px — focal length, rescaled as f·(img_w/640) for other resolutions
y₂, c_y — box bottom edge and image vertical centre
W = wpx · ( d / cos((x_c − c_x)/f) ) / f
Horizontal slant correction — a pothole at the frame edge is further away than one dead centre at the same y, so distance is divided by the cosine of its horizontal angular offset before back-projecting width.
hextent = d(y₁) − d(y₂)
Along-road extent from the difference of two ground distances (top vs bottom edge) — this is what stands in for depth, since true depth is unobservable monocularly.
⚠️
Own this limitation before they ask. Flat-ground planarity, fixed calibration, and no true depth are real constraints. Frame it as: metric estimates are relative and consistent, which is sufficient for ranking roads — absolute millimetre accuracy would need stereo or LiDAR, which is explicitly out of scope for a commodity-dashcam deployment.

Four-stage geometric false-positive cascade

Raw YOLO output on road footage produces predictable false positives — sky patches, tyre marks, tar seams, windshield wipers. Rather than raising the confidence threshold (which kills recall on genuine small potholes), four domain-prior filters run before scoring:

#FilterRuleRejects
1Confidenceconf ≥ 0.15Noise floor
2Horizony₂ ≥ 0.30·H, y₁ ≥ 0.20·HSky, clouds, distant hills
3Aspect0.40 ≤ w/h ≤ 6.0Tyre tracks, poles, seams
4Scale / areaw≥10px, h≥8px, area≥80px², ≤30% frameSpeckle noise, wipers
🎯
The insight worth stating in the paper: perspective foreshortening means genuine road potholes are always wide and flat in image space (aspect > 1), while the common false positives are narrow-tall or span the frame. Geometry does the filtering that confidence alone can't.
05

Road Health Index

The headline number Core contribution #2
Health = 100 − (15·nsevere + 6·nmoderate + 2·nminor)  clamped to [0,100]
Weights are deliberately super-linear across classes — one severe pothole (15 pts) outweighs seven minor ones (14 pts). This encodes the maintenance reality that a single deep pothole causes more vehicle damage and liability than many shallow ones, so a road with one severe defect ranks for repair above a road with scattered minor wear.

Every scan gets one number out of 100. It makes two different roads instantly comparable, which is what a maintenance department actually needs.

This is the output that makes the system decision-useful rather than merely descriptive. A detector emits boxes; a works department needs a ranked list. The index is the bridge, and it is the second thing the paper claims.

06

Engineering Decisions

Built to not fall over Deployability argument
Non-blocking video pipeline

Long videos process in the background — the page stays responsive and shows a live progress bar instead of freezing.

Upload returns a scan_id immediately with status queued; processing runs in a FastAPI BackgroundTask that writes progress every 10 frames. Frontend polls. No request ever holds a connection open for the length of a video.

Zero-crash database fallback

If the cloud database is unreachable, the system silently switches to local storage and keeps working.

Every DB call is gated on test_db_connection(), which caches a successful ping for 5 s and enforces a 30 s retry cooldown when down — so a dead Atlas cluster costs one 2 s timeout, not one per request. All reads/writes have a mirrored JSON-file implementation.

Track-level severity aggregation

Naive per-frame counting inflates totals by the frame count. Detections are grouped by ByteTrack ID and collapsed to one record per physical pothole, with a max-severity-wins upgrade policy — if a pothole is ever scored SEVERE in any frame, it stays SEVERE. Conservative by design: under-reporting a hazard is the worse error.

Browser-playable output encoding

OpenCV's VideoWriter emits containers most browsers refuse. Output is written through imageio's FFmpeg bridge with libx264 / yuv420p, then validated post-write (file exists, non-zero, reopenable) before the scan is marked complete.

Conference Paper Plan

Structure & positioning
Novelty claims — ranked
  1. Monocular metric severity estimation Recovering pothole dimensions in centimetres from a single commodity dashcam via pinhole projection under a flat-ground prior — no stereo, no depth sensor, no per-vehicle calibration rig. This is the paper's centre of gravity.
  2. Multi-factor weighted severity scoring Fusing physical size, depth proxy, shape regularity, detector confidence and temporal persistence into one interpretable score — versus the single-threshold approach typical in prior work.
  3. Geometry-driven false-positive suppression A four-stage cascade using perspective priors specific to road scenes, preserving recall at a low confidence threshold where a naive threshold raise would not.
  4. Road Health Index A scan-level 0–100 metric converting detections into a ranked repair priority — the decision-useful output layer.
  5. Deployed full-stack reference implementation Not a notebook. A running system with async processing, persistence, offline degradation and an operator dashboard.
Suggested section outline
  • I. Introduction — reactive maintenance, the "where but not how bad" gap
  • II. Related Work — pothole detection (CNN/YOLO era), vibration & accelerometer methods, LiDAR/stereo profiling; position yours as the low-cost monocular middle ground
  • III. Methodology — A. Detection backbone · B. Geometric filtering · C. Monocular metric estimation · D. Severity fusion · E. Health index
  • IV. System Architecture — pipeline, async design, persistence
  • V. Experiments — detector metrics + severity validation table
  • VI. Results & Discussion
  • VII. Limitations & Future Work
Limitations to state yourself
  • Flat-ground assumption — breaks on inclines, banking, crowned roads
  • Fixed calibration — H and θ hardcoded; different vehicle geometry shifts all estimates
  • No true depth — along-road extent proxies for depth; a shallow wide patch and a deep one score alike
  • Dataset scale — 535 training images; generalisation across climates, road materials and lighting is unproven
  • Simulated GPS — video trajectory is interpolated, not from a real GPS feed
  • Severity unvalidated — no ground-truth measurement study yet
✅
Naming these yourself reads as rigour and removes them as attack surface in review.
Future work — the natural next paper
  • Auto-calibration from vanishing-point estimation, removing hardcoded H and θ
  • Monocular depth network (MiDaS / Depth-Anything) to replace the extent-based depth proxy with real depth
  • Real GPS + IMU fusion for true geotagging and slope compensation
  • Segmentation head for pothole area in m² rather than bounding-box approximation
  • Edge deployment — quantised ONNX/TensorRT on a Jetson-class device for in-vehicle inference

Questions You'll Get

Panel Reviewer-level
Why YOLO and not another detector?
Single-pass architecture — one forward pass per frame, which is what makes frame-by-frame video processing viable. The nano variant keeps it light enough to run without a dedicated GPU.
How is severity decided?
Several signals are combined — the pothole's real-world size, its shape, the model's confidence, and how consistently it appears across frames — into one score that maps to Minor, Moderate or Severe.
Weighted linear fusion of five normalised factors — 0.45 physical size, 0.20 depth proxy, 0.15 confidence, 0.10 aspect regularity, 0.10 temporal persistence — thresholded at 0.70 for SEVERE and 0.45 for MODERATE.
How do you get real-world size from a flat 2D image?
Using the camera's known mounting geometry — its height above the road and its downward tilt — the system works backwards from where a pothole sits in the frame to roughly how far away and how wide it is.
Pinhole model with a flat-ground prior. The box's bottom edge is treated as the road contact point, giving ground distance d = H/tan(θ + (y₂−c_y)/f) with H=1.3 m, θ=18°, f=800 px. Width back-projects as w_px·d_slant/f, with a cosine correction for horizontal angular offset.
What happens if the database goes down?
Automatic fallback to local JSON storage — every database operation has a mirrored file-based path. Connection state is cached (5 s ping TTL, 30 s retry cooldown) so a dead cluster costs one timeout, not one per request.
Who would actually use this?
Municipal road authorities. Mount dashcams on vehicles already driving their routes — buses, maintenance trucks — and the system produces a ranked list of which roads need repair first, plus a map of where the damage is concentrated.
Why not just use pixel area for severity?
Pixel area is distance-dependent — a small pothole close to the camera occupies more pixels than a large one further away, so it inverts the ranking you actually want. Metric estimation is what makes severity comparable across a frame and across scans. This is the point the whole paper turns on.
Your recall is 0.764 — you're missing a quarter of them.
Correct, and concentrated in small/distant instances. Mitigated two ways: the confidence threshold is deliberately low at 0.15 to favour recall, and the geometric cascade recovers the precision that would otherwise cost. For a prioritisation use-case, missing a minor distant pothole is far cheaper than missing a severe near one — and severe instances are large, which is where recall is strongest.
How do you avoid counting the same pothole in every frame?
ByteTrack assigns persistent IDs across frames. Detections are grouped by track ID and collapsed to one record per physical pothole, with a max-severity-wins policy — worst observed classification is retained. Deliberately conservative: under-reporting a hazard is the costlier error.
Have you validated the centimetre estimates against ground truth?
Answer honestly — not yet. Say the validation study is in progress: hand-measured widths on ~30 real potholes, reported as mean absolute error. Do not overclaim here; a reviewer will catch it, and "in progress with a defined protocol" reads far better than a vague assertion of accuracy.
What breaks your flat-ground assumption?
Inclines, road banking, crowning, and speed bumps — anything violating planarity introduces systematic distance error, which propagates to width. Bounded in practice because the horizon filter already discards the far field where the error grows worst. Auto-calibration via vanishing-point estimation is the stated fix in future work.
